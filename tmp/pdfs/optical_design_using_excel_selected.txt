Two-Dimensional Ray Tracing
In this chapter, we will examine a method for calculating the degree of aberration in an optical system,
using the ray tracing technique. Ray tracing is a straightforward task, if we follow a few simple steps.
First, we begin by drawing the rays emitted from the object, and then we calculate the ray paths. We
do this by calculating the ray heights for rays entering the lens and calculating the slopes of the rays
exiting the boundary surface, applying the law of refraction (commonly known as Snell’s law9). After
repeating this process for each boundary surface, we can finally derive the ray heights on the image plane.
If the optical system has zero aberration, then all of the rays emitted from the (point) object should reach
the image plane, converging precisely at a single point. The object and image are referred to as conjugate
points. In a real-life optical system, however, there is always a small degree of aberration. Hence the rays
on the image plane do not converge exactly at one point, but are instead dispersed within a small radius
of the point where they would converge under ideal conditions.
By calculating the optical path lengths (OPLs) between the boundary surfaces of the lenses, we can
also obtain the cumulative OPL from the object to the reference wave front, which is a sphere whose
center of curvature is situated at the ideal image point. (The reference wave front is the wave front when
an optical system has no aberration.) We can then use the cumulative OPLs to calculate the wave front
aberration of the optical system.
4.1 Ray Tracing for a Spherical Surface
In an optical system consisting of multiple lenses, we can designate the axis along which we measure
the ray height as they-axis and the direction of ray propagation as thez-axis, and we can then proceed as
follows. First, we begin with an object point at an arbitrary height w0 and a ray emitted from the object,
at an arbitrary angle𝜃0 to the optical axis (i.e., thez-axis). We then calculate the ray heightw1, where the

applying Snell’s law. Next, we calculate the ray heightw2 for the second surface and the ray slope angle
𝜃2 for the ray exiting the second surface. If we repeat this process, we finally obtain the ray heightwn on

a much more complex multi-lens system. Here, we shall examine an aspheric lens which is used in LD

optical path length compensationΔLi andΔLo and thatΔLo will be referenced by the “OPL” cell, in the
next calculation unit. Thus the OPL cells next to an aberration-free lens unit should be different from the

4.7.2.3 Phase (Difference) on the Image Plane and Wave-Front Aberration
The phase (difference) on the image plane shows the differences in the cumulative optical path lengths
of the rays from the object to the image plane (we use a positive sign when the phase leads that of the
principal ray).
On the other hand, the wave-front aberration is the phase difference between the wave front after
passing through the last lens and the reference wave front (the wave front when the optical system has
no aberration). When the optical system has no aberration, rays emitted from the object point diverge
(forming a spherical wave front), and then pass through the optical system. After passing through the
last lens, the rays (which have a spherical wave front) will converge precisely at a single point: the ideal
image point. Thus the center of curvature for the reference wave front should be located at the ideal image
point, and the radius can have any value which is significantly greater than the wavelength𝜆.
Figure 4.15 illustrates the principles of wave-front aberration. S0 is the reference wave-front surface
exiting the last lens, which includes the center of the exiting pupil C, and whose center of curvature is
located at the ideal image point Q0 (x0,y0,0). S′is the surface of the wave front exiting the last lens, which
includes C, and Q( x,y,z) is its image point. In Figure 4.15 the 𝜁-axis and the z-axis are situated on the
optical axis, and d is the distance between the𝜉–𝜂plane (the exiting plane) and the x–y plane (the ideal
image plane). A0 (𝜉0,𝜂0,𝜁0) is the point of intersection between the exiting ray and the reference wave
front S0, and A′(𝜉′,𝜂′,𝜁′) is the point of intersection between the exiting ray and the wave front S′.

in the figure depict various wave-front surfaces, over which the light beam will be in phase. The beam
radius wz can be defined as the radius at which the electric field and irradiance drop to 1/ e and 1/e2 of

We have discussed the characteristics of Gaussian beams at considerable length here. The key differ-
ences between Gaussian beam theory and geometrical optics are as follows:
1. Geometrical optics postulates an infinitesimal focal point. In practice, however, beams cannot be
focused onto a spot with an infinitesimal radius; they have a finite, minimum size at the beam waist,
as shown in the preceding discussion of Gaussian beam theory.
2. In geometrical optics, a collimated beam can maintain a constant diameter over an infinite range. In
practice, all beams – including collimated beams – have a beam waist. As we move away from the
waist, the diameter of the beam gradually increases.
3. We can precisely specify a Gaussian beam, simply by specifying its wavelength and the diameter of
the beam waist.
4. Alternatively, we can obtain the beam waist radius w0 and the beam waist distance z0 from the beam
radius w and the radius of curvatureR of the spherical wave front at an arbitrary position (with a fixed
wavelength).
